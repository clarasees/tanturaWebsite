Life in Tantura

The Architecture of the Shore

Tantura was a village built from the very land it stood upon: stone houses were crafted from local kurkar—the white sandstone of the Mediterranean—and mud, creating a shell architecture that mirrored the coastline.

This was a place of vibrant communal life and longevity, anchored by institutions like the boys' school founded in 1889 and the girls' school established in 1937. These structures formed the foundation of a society that valued education and maintained a permanent, sophisticated bond with the Mediterranean.

Knowledge of the Sea

The sea served as the primary highway and lifeblood for the people of Tantura. For generations, the village held a sovereign and indigenous knowledge of these waters.

Lineages of the Sea: Families such as the Abd al-Mu'ti and Al-Yahya were the preeminent masters of the sea. They held specialized knowledge of fourteen distinct fish species, ranging from the prized Al-Marjan (Red Mullet) to the Al-Sardin.

The Art of the Net: The fishermen perfected the Shabaka Fardiya—the individual hand-woven net—a technique crafted specifically for the shallow, rocky reefs of the Tantura shore.

The Port of Al-Mina: The harbor was a vital link in a regional economy. Large sailboats known as Schuners carried harvests of grain and watermelons from the fertile Palestinian plains to the markets of Lebanon and Egypt.

The 'Araysh: Coastal huts and cafés along the shore served as the social heart of maritime life. These were the diwans of the sea, where traders from Haifa met with local fishermen to share news and sustain the community's maritime culture.

Sumoud: Daily Presence and the Practice of Return

The memory of Tantura is not confined to the archives; it lives in the physical proximity of its neighboring Palestinian towns. For the families of Tantura, many of whom were displaced to Fureidis (the "Little Paradise") and Jisr al-Zarqa (the "Blue Bridge"), the remnants of the village remain accessible by beach.

Fureidis and Jisr al-Zarqa serve as the primary anchors for the displaced community. In Jisr al-Zarqa—the only remaining coastal Palestinian village in the region—the maritime heritage continues through an active fishing culture that carries forward the traditions of pre-1948 Tantura. In Fureidis, the community has maintained its social fabric and lineages for over seventy years, living in the immediate shadow of their ancestral lands.

Daily Reclamation

While the state has utilized the Absentee Property Law to prevent Palestinians from returning as residents or landowners, the beach of Tantura remains a public space. This opening has allowed for a "daily return" that no law can fully prohibit. Every day, families from Fureidis and Jisr al-Zarqa return to the waters of Tantura to fish, swim, and gather as a persistent, physical reclamation of space.

By inhabiting the beach alongside resort guests, the community forces a reckoning. Their presence disrupts the "seamless landscape" of Israeli leisure, compelling others to witness a living Palestinian reality that refuses to be historicized or hidden beneath a car park.

Collective Organizing and the Annual Horizon

This daily, individual presence provides the foundation for larger, collective acts of advocacy and memory led by the "Filastiniyyat" association and village committees.

Organized Remembrance: The annual Martyrs' March and the Communal Iftars are the culmination of this daily persistence. Every year, crowds gather on the sands of Tantura to walk the paths of their ancestors. When the Maghrib call to prayer sounds over the coast, it marks a moment of profound spiritual reconnection. Breaking the fast together on the village lands is a collective enactment of presence where the joy of gathering and the memory of the past exist as one.

The Weight of Memory: For Palestinians, remembering the violence of the Nakba is not a choice but a daily reality they are forced to endure. By gathering in Tantura, the burden of memory is shifted to a public consciousness, requiring a confrontation with the ongoing nature of their displacement.

Advocacy and the Protection of Memory

The Tantura Displaced Persons Committee, led by Sami Al-Ali, continues the vital work of protecting the village heritage and the dignity of those who were lost.

Forensic Truth: At the request of the families, a professional investigation by Forensic Architecture identified the exact locations of mass graves. Using the surviving Al-Yahya house as a physical baseline, this work provided a precise map of the village history, grounded in the physical reality of the site.

Protecting the Graves: The Committee stands firmly against any attempts to build tourist resorts or facilities over the resting places of the villagers. Their advocacy is a demand for the respect and official demarcation of these sites—to fence, sign, and honor these graves so that families can visit and remember their martyrs with dignity.

A Legacy Carried Forward

The history of Tantura is a line that runs through families and through practice. From the height of Burj Hill to the clarity of the Horse Wash, the village lives in the songs that are still sung and the way the water is still read. The descendants of Tantura remain the keepers of this maritime lineage. By documenting this living presence and standing guard over the history of the site, the community ensures that Tantura remains a place of belonging and a destination for future generations.

About

Tantura was a Palestinian fishing village on the Carmel Coast, known for its ancient port and rich history stretching back to the Bronze Age.

This site explores the village's landscape, life, and memory.
